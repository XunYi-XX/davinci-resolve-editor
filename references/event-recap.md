# Event-Recap Emotional Documentary Montage

For a short culture-festival/social recap, use
[cultural-quick-cut.md](cultural-quick-cut.md). For analysis of a finished
timeline, use [style-audit.md](style-audit.md).

Use this framework for reunions, anniversaries, trips, school or company events,
ceremonies, and other real-event films where the edit should feel emotionally
shaped without losing documentary credibility. It is broader than the
lyrical-film section: the first half can be a lively visual montage, while the
ending can hand over to long live-sound coverage.

## Narrative Shape

A useful adaptable arc is:

1. **Context** — Establish place and scale with aerial, exterior, or
   environmental shots. Compress stable traversals with speed changes only when
   the subject remains readable.
2. **Human entry** — Move from space into people, greetings, walking, gestures,
   and reactions.
3. **Place and memory evidence** — Intercut group views with hands pointing,
   faces watching, archival displays, certificates, objects, and interaction.
4. **Collective proof** — Use banners, group photos, landmarks, and repeated
   views of the same group, but change place, framing, or emotional function.
5. **Social climax** — Alternate speakers, listeners, reactions, laughing, and
   toasts so that the soundtrack has visible ownership.
6. **Live-sound release** — Let the strongest human interaction breathe in
   longer shots, then resolve picture, music, ambience, and the closing card
   together.

Do not force a literal chronology when a nearby moment better serves the
narrative beat. Preserve factual clarity, but sequence by information and
emotion rather than file creation time.

## Cut by Information, Not Decoration

Before selecting a new shot, identify the one job it performs:

- places the viewer in space;
- introduces a person or relationship;
- shows an action beginning or completing;
- supplies a reaction, eyeline, or gesture;
- proves the occasion with a banner, certificate, display, or group;
- reveals a detail that would be unreadable in the wide shot.

A strong recurring grammar is:

`wide context → medium interaction → gesture or face → object/detail → listener
reaction → new wide or group`

Choose source handles around a readable action, expression, pointing gesture,
toast, or line of speech. Avoid using the first attractive frame merely because
it is easy to locate.

## Rhythm Calibration

Use the event's pressure, not a fixed average:

- Exhibition, sightseeing, greetings, and reaction montages often work around
  1–4 seconds per shot.
- Ceremonial proof, certificates, banners, emotional faces, and establishing
  shots often need 4–8 seconds or more.
- A dense montage can rise to roughly 25–30 cuts per minute, followed by a
  deliberately sparse release.
- Live speeches, performances, or group interaction may justify 15–40 second
  shots when composition, subjects, or sound evolve internally.
- Keep very short shots purposeful: use them for a specific gesture, reaction,
  punchline, musical hit, or accumulation.

A frame-audited five-and-a-half-minute reunion reference contained 103 real
shots with a 3.29-second mean and 2.44-second median, but its final 68 seconds
used only three shots. That contrast is more useful than the average itself:
the edit moves from montage density to live-sound release. Treat these figures
as calibration evidence, not quotas.

## Long-Source Mining

For event recordings lasting several minutes:

1. Review and mark the usable event peaks before editing: speech lines,
   laughter, applause, toasts, gestures, reactions, or changes in group
   formation.
2. Extract narrow, non-contiguous ranges around those peaks.
3. Reuse one long source at different points only when each range contributes a
   new beat, reaction, or escalation.
4. Keep enough source handles to choose the exact action onset and release.
5. Prefer a synchronized reaction shot after a toast or statement over another
   generic view of the same speaker.

One source can therefore supply the speaker, a listener reaction, and a toast
without functioning as three interchangeable filler shots.

## Speed Changes

- Speed up only movement whose geography or subject stays legible. A stable
  aerial path or long corridor traversal is a better candidate than busy human
  interaction.
- Use slow motion for a ceremony, certificate, handshake, or emotional beat
  where added duration reveals weight rather than novelty.
- A reverse-speed reveal can work once as a structural transition, for example
  opening a decorative screen into the next room.
- Keep normal speed as the default. Sparse speed changes retain meaning.

## Chapter Graphics

- Use a location or chapter title for a group of shots, not a new caption on
  every cut.
- A lower third lasting roughly 8–12 seconds can bridge several short shots and
  keep the location stable while the edit maintains motion.
- Match the graphic language to the event. Restrained institutional labels,
  red-white anniversary motifs, dates, and place names can reinforce purpose;
  they should not cover primary faces or important reactions.
- Distinguish timeline overlays from graphics already baked into source
  footage. Record both, but treat only timeline items as editable patterns.

## Audio Handoff

- Let music create continuity through the opening and montage.
- Introduce selected location sound when the viewer needs presence: greeting,
  laughter, footsteps, applause, a speech line, or a toast.
- In the social climax, alternate music with live voices and reactions instead
  of keeping every layer equally prominent.
- Near the ending, let live sound take the foreground. Long shots are justified
  when laughter, conversation, applause, or performance carries the emotion.
- Reusing the same musical phrase at the opening and coda can create a bookend
  when the phrase supports the final image and does not force an abrupt loop.
- Use overlap or a short fade to make the handoff intentional. Avoid unrelated
  hard silences unless silence is the emotional beat.

## Event-Recap Review Questions

- Does the opening establish enough place before people and details dominate?
- Does every cut add space, person, action, reaction, object, or group evidence?
- Are repeated banners or group photos changing location or emotional meaning?
- Does the montage eventually release into a genuine live moment?
- Do long takes have internal action, composition change, or meaningful sound?
- Are speed changes reserved for stable geometry, ceremony, or one structural
  reveal?
- Do chapter graphics support multiple shots without becoming shot labels?
- Does live sound progressively become more important toward the ending?
- Does the final card feel motivated by the preceding picture and sound?
