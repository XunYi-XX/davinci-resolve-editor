# Narrative and Emotional Editing Reference

Read this reference when the user asks for editorial judgment rather than a
mechanical timeline operation: mood-led montage, an emotional event recap,
style analysis, or a request described as `抒情情绪片` (a lyrical emotional
film). Treat it as a starting framework, not a mandatory sequence.

For basketball games and sports-event recaps, also read
[basketball-event-recap.md](basketball-event-recap.md). It covers action-range
selection, participation and supporter coverage, variable cut density, and
evidence-based music analysis.

## Lyrical Emotional Film

A lyrical emotional film creates emotion through contrast and progression:
clear spatial context, human presence, measured breathing room, restrained
graphics, and a deliberate relationship between music and location sound.
Fast cutting is valid when it serves accumulation rather than constant
stimulation.

### Story Arc

1. **Atmosphere** — Establish place, time, and emotional tone with aerial,
   environmental, or detail shots. Compress slow setup with selective speed
   changes or short cuts when the material supports it.
2. **Human entry** — Move from space into people and action. Use varied shot
   sizes and camera positions to maintain forward motion.
3. **Emotional expansion** — Alternate active sequences with quieter looks,
   reactions, relationship details, names, or location sound. Let important
   moments breathe.
4. **Release and coda** — Let the strongest emotional or physical beat resolve,
   then hand the scene from location sound to music. Use longer dissolves or
   held frames when the footage supports a clear ending.

### Rhythm

- Use a variable rhythm. A useful starting range for a lyrical event piece is
  roughly 2–6 seconds for most shots, 1–2 seconds for action accents, and
  6–30 seconds for establishing, reaction, or closing shots.
- Keep very short shots purposeful: use them to accelerate a phrase, hit an
  action, or mark a musical accent.
- Do not make every shot the same length. Group short shots into a rise, then
  allow a longer shot to release tension.
- Judge rhythm against the music or the natural action rather than forcing an
  exact average.

### Shot Grammar

- Use wide, medium, and close views to change information and emotional
  distance, not merely for visual variety.
- Prefer cuts that preserve action direction, eyeline, gesture, or an audible
  beat. Match motion when the material permits.
- Use aerial shots for geography and scale; handheld shots for presence and
  immediacy; faces and hands for emotional detail.
- Repeated source material is acceptable when different sections serve
  different narrative functions. Do not repeat a visually identical beat
  without a new meaning.

### Transitions

- Default to hard cuts for continuity and honesty.
- Reserve dissolve transitions for a genuine time jump, section change,
  emotional softening, or ending.
- A useful range is roughly 0.5–1 second inside a flowing sequence and 1–2
  seconds at an important turn or final release.
- Avoid decorating every cut. Transition frequency should be much lower than
  cut frequency.

### Titles and Graphics

- Use graphics to establish a person, chapter, date, or essential context.
- Keep overlays sparse and hold them long enough to read without covering an
  important facial expression.
- Keep the narrative spine on V1. Place temporary titles or name straps on a
  higher track. Leave additional tracks empty until they have a job.

### Sound

- Music usually carries emotional continuity across the visual edit.
- Use location sound selectively to create presence: footsteps, laughter,
  voices, hands, movement, room tone, or a key spoken line.
- Let music yield to important speech or an emotional sound event, then return
  when the visual section needs momentum or release.
- Brief overlaps can hide an audible handoff. Avoid a hard, unexplained silence
  unless silence is the intended emotional beat.
- Do not keep every layer at full emphasis. Contrast creates emotion.

### Review Questions

- Does the opening establish place and tone before asking the viewer to feel?
- Does the piece move from context to people, action, and emotional detail?
- Does shot length change with the emotional pressure?
- Are cuts motivated by action, eyeline, gesture, sound, or musical movement?
- Are dissolves rare and meaningful?
- Do music and location sound have a clear hierarchy?
- Does the ending resolve the picture, music, and strongest emotional beat
  together?

## Event-Recap Emotional Documentary Montage

Use this framework for reunions, anniversaries, trips, school or company events,
ceremonies, and other real-event films where the edit should feel emotionally
shaped without losing documentary credibility. It is broader than the
lyrical-film section: the first half can be a lively visual montage, while the
ending can hand over to long live-sound coverage.

### Narrative Shape

A useful adaptable arc is:

1. **Context** — Establish place and scale with aerial, exterior, or
   environmental shots. Compress stable traversals with speed changes only when
   the subject remains readable.
2. **Human entry** — Move from space into people, greetings, walking, gestures,
   and reactions.
3. **Place and memory evidence** — Intercut group views with hands pointing,
   faces watching, archival displays, certificates, objects, and interaction.
4. **Collective proof** — Use banners, group photos, landmarks, and repeated
   views of the same group, but change place, framing, or emotional function.
5. **Social climax** — Alternate speakers, listeners, reactions, laughing, and
   toasts so that the soundtrack has visible ownership.
6. **Live-sound release** — Let the strongest human interaction breathe in
   longer shots, then resolve picture, music, ambience, and the closing card
   together.

Do not force a literal chronology when a nearby moment better serves the
narrative beat. Preserve factual clarity, but sequence by information and
emotion rather than file creation time.

### Cut by Information, Not Decoration

Before selecting a new shot, identify the one job it performs:

- places the viewer in space;
- introduces a person or relationship;
- shows an action beginning or completing;
- supplies a reaction, eyeline, or gesture;
- proves the occasion with a banner, certificate, display, or group;
- reveals a detail that would be unreadable in the wide shot.

A strong recurring grammar is:

`wide context → medium interaction → gesture or face → object/detail → listener
reaction → new wide or group`

Choose source handles around a readable action, expression, pointing gesture,
toast, or line of speech. Avoid using the first attractive frame merely because
it is easy to locate.

### Rhythm Calibration

Use the event's pressure, not a fixed average:

- Exhibition, sightseeing, greetings, and reaction montages often work around
  1–4 seconds per shot.
- Ceremonial proof, certificates, banners, emotional faces, and establishing
  shots often need 4–8 seconds or more.
- A dense montage can rise to roughly 25–30 cuts per minute, followed by a
  deliberately sparse release.
- Live speeches, performances, or group interaction may justify 15–40 second
  shots when composition, subjects, or sound evolve internally.
- Keep very short shots purposeful: use them for a specific gesture, reaction,
  punchline, musical hit, or accumulation.

A frame-audited five-and-a-half-minute reunion reference contained 103 real
shots with a 3.29-second mean and 2.44-second median, but its final 68 seconds
used only three shots. That contrast is more useful than the average itself:
the edit moves from montage density to live-sound release. Treat these figures
as calibration evidence, not quotas.

### Long-Source Mining

For event recordings lasting several minutes:

1. Review and mark the usable event peaks before editing: speech lines,
   laughter, applause, toasts, gestures, reactions, or changes in group
   formation.
2. Extract narrow, non-contiguous ranges around those peaks.
3. Reuse one long source at different points only when each range contributes a
   new beat, reaction, or escalation.
4. Keep enough source handles to choose the exact action onset and release.
5. Prefer a synchronized reaction shot after a toast or statement over another
   generic view of the same speaker.

One source can therefore supply the speaker, a listener reaction, and a toast
without functioning as three interchangeable filler shots.

### Speed Changes

- Speed up only movement whose geography or subject stays legible. A stable
  aerial path or long corridor traversal is a better candidate than busy human
  interaction.
- Use slow motion for a ceremony, certificate, handshake, or emotional beat
  where added duration reveals weight rather than novelty.
- A reverse-speed reveal can work once as a structural transition, for example
  opening a decorative screen into the next room.
- Keep normal speed as the default. Sparse speed changes retain meaning.

### Chapter Graphics

- Use a location or chapter title for a group of shots, not a new caption on
  every cut.
- A lower third lasting roughly 8–12 seconds can bridge several short shots and
  keep the location stable while the edit maintains motion.
- Match the graphic language to the event. Restrained institutional labels,
  red-white anniversary motifs, dates, and place names can reinforce purpose;
  they should not cover primary faces or important reactions.
- Distinguish timeline overlays from graphics already baked into source
  footage. Record both, but treat only timeline items as editable patterns.

### Audio Handoff

- Let music create continuity through the opening and montage.
- Introduce selected location sound when the viewer needs presence: greeting,
  laughter, footsteps, applause, a speech line, or a toast.
- In the social climax, alternate music with live voices and reactions instead
  of keeping every layer equally prominent.
- Near the ending, let live sound take the foreground. Long shots are justified
  when laughter, conversation, applause, or performance carries the emotion.
- Reusing the same musical phrase at the opening and coda can create a bookend
  when the phrase supports the final image and does not force an abrupt loop.
- Use overlap or a short fade to make the handoff intentional. Avoid unrelated
  hard silences unless silence is the emotional beat.

### Event-Recap Review Questions

- Does the opening establish enough place before people and details dominate?
- Does every cut add space, person, action, reaction, object, or group evidence?
- Are repeated banners or group photos changing location or emotional meaning?
- Does the montage eventually release into a genuine live moment?
- Do long takes have internal action, composition change, or meaningful sound?
- Are speed changes reserved for stable geometry, ceremony, or one structural
  reveal?
- Do chapter graphics support multiple shots without becoming shot labels?
- Does live sound progressively become more important toward the ending?
- Does the final card feel motivated by the preceding picture and sound?

### Cultural-Event Quick-Cut Variant

Use this variant for a 30–90 second culture festival, exhibition, campus open
day, tourism event, or social-media recap. It is music-led and experience-led
rather than a complete record of the programme.

#### Narrative Shape

A useful adaptable sequence is:

`scale/context → tactile detail → participation → cultural craft → performance
→ group farewell → hard finish`

1. Open with a wide aerial or space-establishing view and, if appropriate, one
   event-title overlay.
2. Move quickly into hands, props, decorations, food, paper, ink, instruments,
   or other tactile detail. In the first 5–10 seconds, let the viewer feel the
   event before explaining its schedule.
3. Show participation and interaction at stalls, workshops, or displays.
4. Introduce cultural expression through craft, writing, music, costume,
   dance, or performance.
5. Let the strongest stage or group performance carry the longest shots.
6. End with a rapid run of people waving, smiling, or looking to camera. Use a
   short black or graphic card rather than lingering on the last group photo.

#### Shot Selection

- Prefer a complete readable gesture: a sticker being pressed, a brush
  reaching its stroke, an instrument being played, or a group beginning a wave.
- Alternate macro detail with wide geography. A fast cut stays legible when the
  viewer receives a new scale, subject, or action, not merely a new angle.
- Use close-ups of hands and materials for authenticity; use faces and group
  reactions for participation and emotion.
- Prefer many distinct source shots over repeated fragments of one source.
  Quick-cut coverage usually benefits more from breadth than long-source
  mining.
- Even short source clips may need further trimming. Select the action peak
  rather than using the whole camera take.
- Match repeated visual motifs such as festival colours, costumes, props, or
  waving gestures so rapid cuts retain a coherent identity.

#### Rhythm

- A useful range is roughly 1.5–4 seconds for the main body, with short
  0.6–1.5 second bursts for final waves, reactions, or musical accents.
- Do not make the entire cut uniformly fast. Hold a performance wide, an
  instrument action, or a particularly legible cultural detail slightly longer.
- A verified 72-second cultural-event quick-cut contained 29 source shots with
  a 2.48-second mean and 2.24-second median. Its final 6.5-second group-wave
  sequence averaged about 1.3 seconds per shot. Treat these as calibration
  evidence, not quotas.
- Coordinate cuts with musical energy or phrase changes. Do not claim exact beat
  synchronization unless beat positions and cut distances have been measured.

#### Speed and Transitions

- Use speed ramps primarily on stable aerial or spatial moves. In the verified
  reference, three aerial/stage shots used roughly 3×–8× compression.
- A single crash zoom can work as the opening transition from aerial geography
  into tactile detail. Do not add crash zooms at every section.
- A distinctive quick-cut pattern can combine many micro-dissolves with a few
  long blends:
  - 0.2–0.4 second dissolves smooth motion while still feeling like cuts;
  - 1.5–2.5 second dissolves may bridge continuous craft actions such as
    writing or paper-making;
  - default to hard cuts when the action cut is already clear.
- Distinguish transition objects from solid end cards. A 0.3–0.5 second black
  or graphic card can create an intentional stop after the final group wave.

#### Opening Graphics and Sound

- A short full-frame title, title pan, or event graphic may sit on a higher
  video track over the aerial for roughly 2–4 seconds.
- Keep the event visual motif unified through source content when possible:
  repeated festival colours, stage banners, costumes, and signage can unify
  rapid cuts without applying a uniform filter.
- A continuous music bed often carries the quick cut. Add short risers only at
  a structural setup such as the title launch or first cut into detail.
- A five-second music fade can support the final wave montage and black card.
- If the intended form has no live-sound release, do not force location audio
  into the end; music-led social cuts may end directly.

#### Comparing Quick-Cut Versions

When two timelines contain similar material, compare:

- track and clip enabled state;
- true last picture frame versus the timeline or audio tail;
- real-shot count and duration distribution;
- unique media and the one or two added/removed shots;
- source in/out changes, not only timeline duration;
- speed ratio and transition-type changes;
- which musical bed is enabled;
- final card and fade behavior.

A visually shorter revision should show where time was removed. In a verified
refinement, one low-information character cutaway was removed, a five-second
craft action was reduced to about two seconds, instrument and stage shots were
lengthened, and the ending gained a black card. This is a useful example of
deleting weak information while preserving performance breathing room.

## Common Failure Modes

- Cutting every source to the same duration.
- Treating constant fast cutting as automatically emotional or cinematic.
- Using transitions on every cut.
- Covering the strongest human moments with continuous graphics.
- Keeping music at full presence while important location sound competes with
  it.
- Sequencing attractive shots without a spatial, human, or emotional arc.

## Extract a Style from an Existing Timeline

When asked to learn from an existing Resolve timeline, default to read-only.
Do not save the project, add markers, duplicate timelines, alter clips, or
export unless the user separately requests those operations.

1. Record the project and timeline names/IDs privately, plus the timeline frame
   rate, duration, current render, and whether the project has unsaved changes.
2. Count items by track and separate real media clips from transition objects
   and generated graphics. Record whether each track and clip is enabled; a
   long muted section may be a discarded or reference version. Query enabled
   state with each target timeline made current, because Resolve may report
   misleading `False` values for a non-current timeline; restore the original
   current timeline and playhead afterward without saving.
3. Calculate shot-length minimum, maximum, mean, median, useful percentiles,
   and counts by duration band. Also calculate cut density by narrative chapter
   rather than relying on one whole-film average.
4. Resolve each source item's actual media frame rate before calculating a
   retiming ratio. Compare source-range duration with timeline duration to
   distinguish normal, slow, fast, and reversed playback.
5. Group source ranges by media ID. Record unique-source count and repeated,
   non-contiguous ranges; these often reveal the editor's long-source mining
   strategy.
6. Sample at least one midpoint frame from every real timeline shot. For
   representative shots and all notable long sources, sample source in, middle,
   and out frames. Verify whether the chosen range starts on an action, reaction,
   gesture, or readable composition.
7. Use visual and sound changes to define chapters, then compute shot count,
   average/median length, and cuts per minute for each chapter. This exposes
   rhythm contrast that whole-timeline statistics hide.
8. Record transition types, exact timeline positions, durations, and what
   narrative change each supports. Separate hard cuts from transition objects.
9. Record overlay and title holds, distinguishing timeline graphics from
   source-baked text. Note unused tracks.
10. Inspect audio layering by track: music beds, location sound, speech,
    overlaps, fades, local gain changes, and where live sound becomes primary.
    Check whether a musical phrase is reused at the opening and coda.
11. Check sequence continuity, source reuse, marker use, and whether the final
    delivered render matches the inspected timeline duration.
12. Re-read project/timeline identity and item counts after frame extraction to
    confirm the analysis remained non-destructive.
13. Generalize only patterns supported by multiple shots or an explicit
    structural function. Label project-specific measurements as evidence, not
    universal rules.
14. If the project has multiple similar timelines, compare enabled state, true
    picture end, shared media IDs, source-range changes, speed ratios,
    transitions, audio beds, and ending cards before deciding which is active.
15. Remove project names, timeline IDs, file paths, faces, and other identifying
    details before publishing the learned style publicly.

When frame extraction is needed, write temporary images outside the project and
the media directories. Do not save the Resolve project merely to produce
analysis frames.

## Application Boundary

Use this reference for editorial recommendations or when the user explicitly
requests a lyrical emotional style. Inspect the current timeline before
changing it. Do not infer permission to alter a user project merely because the
style framework is available, and never present a template timing as more
important than the footage, music, or user's stated intent.
